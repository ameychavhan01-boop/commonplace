## 01 · COLD OPEN

[VISUAL] Black. A ticker board clicks. Corn prices begin to fall, tick by tick, then freeze at a red "LIMIT DOWN" bar. Slow push-in.

Spring, 1971. A young man stands in a brokerage office, watching a price board. Every dollar he has, and twenty thousand dollars borrowed from his mother, rests on a single idea. {C11}

The prices start to fall. He knows he should sell. He doesn't. He watches, hoping the market will turn. Then the board stops moving. The market has hit its daily limit. Trading is frozen, and there is no way out until tomorrow. {C12}

By his own account, he was paralysed. He had all night to think about it, and nothing he could do. {C12}

[VISUAL] Title card sequence: name fades in over the frozen board.

His name was Michael Marcus. Within fifteen years of that morning, he would become one of the most successful traders of his generation. According to Jack Schwager, who interviewed him for the first chapter of the first *Market Wizards* book, Marcus multiplied his company trading account roughly twenty-five hundred times in a decade. {C30}

But the story of Michael Marcus is not really a story about winning. It is a story about losing — repeatedly, painfully, sometimes absurdly — and slowly discovering the rules that allow a trader to survive long enough to win.

This is that story.

## 02 · CHAPTER ONE — THE SCHOLAR

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "CHAPTER ONE · The Scholar". Archival-style texture: campus, 1969 (illustrative, no real photos).

In 1969, Michael Marcus graduated from Johns Hopkins University, Phi Beta Kappa, near the top of his class. He had a doctoral fellowship in psychology waiting for him at Clark University. By his own description, he fully expected to become a professor. {C02}{C03}

Then, through a friend, he met a fellow student called John. John said he could double Marcus's money every two weeks — like clockwork. {C04}

Marcus admitted later that he never even asked how. The idea was so attractive that he didn't want to spoil it by learning too many facts. It is a small confession, but it contains the first lesson of this entire series: the most dangerous moment in a trader's life is the moment he wants something to be true. {C04}

[VISUAL] Ledger animation: "$1,000 savings" · "Adviser: $30/week".

He hired John as his commodity adviser for thirty dollars a week — with, Marcus remembered, the occasional bag of potato chips. He put up about a thousand dollars of savings. {C04}

Their first trip to a brokerage was to the Reynolds Securities office in Baltimore: mahogany panels, hushed voices, and a great clicking quote board at the front of the room — so far from the visitors' gallery that they watched prices through binoculars. {C04}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "WHAT IS A FUTURES CONTRACT?" Animated contract card: commodity, quantity, delivery month, price. Then "MARGIN": a small deposit controlling a large position; leverage dial.

To understand what happened next, we need one idea. A futures contract is an agreement to buy or sell a fixed quantity of a commodity — corn, wheat, soybean meal — at a set price, for delivery in a future month. Traders don't pay the full value. They put up a small deposit, called margin. That makes futures powerful, and dangerous. A small change in price produces a large change in the trader's account.

When a voice on the loudspeaker recommended buying soybean meal, Marcus turned to his adviser, expecting confidence. Instead, John asked him: "Do you think we should do it?" {C05}

They bought. Prices began to tick down almost immediately. Marcus got out with a loss of about a hundred dollars. Then corn. Then wheat. Each trade lost. They began, Marcus joked, measuring success by how many days it took to lose their money. {C05}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "THE SPREAD THAT COULDN'T LOSE". Two contract months side by side: AUGUST and FEBRUARY pork bellies. A bracket labelled "spread". A second bracket labelled "carrying charge: storage + interest + insurance".

Down to about five hundred dollars, John found a trade he said could not lose. Buy August pork bellies, sell February pork bellies. The price gap between the two months was wider than the cost of storing bellies from August to February. In theory, a trader could take delivery in August, store the product, and deliver it in February — locking in the difference. {C06}

There was a problem. As Marcus learned later, August bellies could not be delivered against the February contract. The arbitrage did not exist. The premise was wrong from the start. {C06}

[VISUAL] Red stamp over the diagram: "NOT DELIVERABLE".

Confident for once, they went out to lunch. When they came back, Marcus was almost wiped out. He would never forget John pounding on the quote board and shouting, "Doesn't anyone want to make a guaranteed profit!" {C06}

[VISUAL] Lower-third lesson card.

There is no such thing as a guaranteed profit. When a trade looks riskless, the risk is usually in the part you don't understand.

Marcus fired his adviser. He scraped together another five hundred dollars, traded silver on his own, and lost that too. Eight trades. Eight losses. {C05}

He never considered quitting. He had always done well at school; he assumed trading was simply a knack he hadn't learned yet. {C05}

## 03 · CHAPTER TWO — THE BLIGHT

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "CHAPTER TWO · The Blight". Cornfield illustration, leaves darkening.

Marcus's father had died when he was fifteen, leaving three thousand dollars in life insurance. Against his mother's objections, Marcus cashed it in. But this time he decided he would learn something first. {C07}

He studied the books of Chester Keltner, a commodity analyst, and subscribed to Keltner's market letter. The first recommendation was to buy wheat. It worked — a gain of about four cents a bushel, two hundred dollars. His first winning trade. {C07}

Then, in the summer of 1970, he bought three contracts of December corn on a Keltner recommendation. {C08}

[VISUAL] Map of the US Corn Belt. Red spreading pattern. Data card: "1970 Southern Corn Leaf Blight · ≈15% of US corn crop lost" — source: USDA/NASA.

That summer, a fungal disease called southern corn leaf blight swept through America's cornfields. Government and research reports later estimated it destroyed roughly fifteen percent of the national corn crop — in some fields, almost everything. {C09}

Corn prices surged. So did Marcus's account. Adding corn, wheat and soybeans — partly on the newsletter, partly on instinct — he finished that summer with about thirty thousand dollars. For a young man from a middle-class family, it was a fortune. {C08}

He was honest about it afterwards. He called the trade "sheer luck." {C08}

[VISUAL] Split-screen: "Skill" vs "Luck". Coin flips sequence.

This is a moment worth pausing on. A beginner who wins big early has a problem that a beginner who loses does not: he may not know which part of the win was skill and which part was luck. Marcus was about to find out.

That autumn he started graduate school in Worcester, Massachusetts. Instead of attending classes, he slipped away to a local brokerage office to trade. In December 1970, he left the program and moved to New York. When people asked what he did, he told them — a little grandly — that he was a speculator. {C10}

[VISUAL] Headline-style card (stylised, not a reproduction): "Will the blight return?"

In the spring of 1971, a theory spread through the grain markets: the blight had survived the winter and would strike again. Marcus decided he would be fully positioned this time. {C11}

He borrowed twenty thousand dollars from his mother, added it to his thirty thousand, and bought as many corn and wheat contracts as fifty thousand dollars of margin would allow. Everything he had. On one idea. {C11}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "LIMIT DOWN". A price track with a floor drawn at the daily limit. Sell orders pile up as red blocks; buyers vanish. Clock icon: "No exit until tomorrow".

For a while, fear of the blight held prices up. Then, as Marcus remembered it, a newspaper article ridiculed the idea — suggesting there was more blight on the trading floor in Chicago than in the cornfields. Corn opened sharply lower and fell to its daily limit. {C12}

Here's what that means. Many futures markets cap how far prices may move in a single day. If the true price — the price where buyers and sellers would meet — lies below that cap, trading effectively stops. There are plenty of sellers, and almost no one willing to buy at the limit. The market is "locked limit-down." A trader who has not already sold is trapped.

Marcus had watched it happen in real time. He knew he should sell on the way down. He froze, hoping for a turn. By the time the market locked, there was no way out. {C12}

The next morning, he sold everything at the opening. He lost his own thirty thousand dollars — and twelve thousand of the twenty thousand his mother had lent him. {C13}

[VISUAL] Lesson card, gold rule: "RISK OF RUIN". Animated bar: one position = 100% of capital.

He called it his lesson in betting his whole stake. But as we'll see, it took him one more near-disaster before the lesson truly took hold.

## 04 · CHAPTER THREE — BEHIND THE GLASS

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "CHAPTER THREE · Behind the Glass". An office with a glass partition; a quote board blurred beyond it.

With a recession on, Marcus went looking for work. He found that he couldn't land even jobs he was well qualified for — and concluded it was because he didn't really want them. When he applied for a position he truly wanted — commodity research analyst at Reynolds Securities — he got it. {C14}

There is a curious connection here. When Marcus left that Reynolds job, his replacement was a young analyst named Jack Schwager — the man who, years later, would interview him for *Market Wizards*. {C14}

At Reynolds, analysts were forbidden to trade. Marcus traded anyway. He borrowed from his mother, his brother and his girlfriend, opened an account at another firm, and communicated with his broker in code. "The sun is out" meant one thing. "The weather is cloudy" meant another. {C14}

[VISUAL] Code-word cards flipping: SUN = ? CLOUDY = ?

Through the glass partition he watched the brokers' board while writing his research reports, trying to keep his face blank whether he was winning or losing. He described the period as a manic-depressive state. He kept losing. {C14}

[VISUAL] Portrait placeholder (no image) — name card: "ED SEYKOTA · MIT, 1969". Data card: "Early computer-tested trend-following systems". Source: Wikipedia/Seykota.

Then, in October 1971, at his broker's office, he met the man he would credit with his success: Ed Seykota. {C15}

Seykota had recently graduated from MIT and was building some of the first computer programs to test and trade technical systems. He was a trend follower: he bought markets that were rising and sold markets that were falling, and stayed with them until the trend changed. {C15}{C16}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "TREND FOLLOWING". A price series; an entry arrow at a breakout; a trailing exit line rising beneath the price; exit when price crosses it. Second panel: many small red losses and a few large gold wins; cumulative curve rising.

The idea is simple to state and brutally hard to practise. You accept many small losses, cutting each one quickly. And you let a few winners run as far as they can. Because the winners are so much larger than the losers, the account can grow even if most trades fail.

Marcus remembered one example. Seykota was short silver while the market slipped lower — half a penny a day, a penny a day. Everyone around him argued silver was cheap and had to go up. Seykota stayed short. His explanation, as Marcus recalled it, was simple: the trend was down, and he would stay short until it changed. {C16}

Marcus learned patience from watching that. But not immediately. He kept losing, even with Seykota beside him. Looking back, he said he wasn't patient enough to wait for a clearly defined situation. {C16}

A second mentor appeared: Amos Hostetter, a semi-retired and highly successful broker at Shearson, who liked Marcus's writing. Hostetter taught the same principles. Two teachers, one message: cut losses, ride winners, be patient. {C16}

By mid-1972, Marcus had run out of money, and out of people willing to lend him any. All he had left, he said, was a stubborn confidence that he could get back on track. {C17}

## 05 · CHAPTER FOUR — THE CEILING THAT BROKE

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "CHAPTER FOUR · The Ceiling That Broke". Data card: "Aug 1971 — Nixon wage-price freeze · Nov 1971–Jan 1973 — Phase II controls". Source: Presidency Project.

To understand Marcus's breakthrough, we need some history. In August 1971, President Nixon froze wages and prices to fight inflation. The freeze was followed by a longer period of official price controls. {S12}

Marcus was earning twelve and a half thousand dollars a year. He saved seven hundred. Not enough to open an account — so a friend added seven hundred more, and Marcus ran the joint account. {C17}

One of the markets he covered as an analyst was plywood. As he recalled it, the plywood price was supposed to be held at a ceiling of one hundred and ten dollars per thousand square feet. With prices near that level, Marcus actually published a bearish view: if prices legally couldn't go above a hundred and ten, there was little to lose by selling there. {C18}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "WHAT PRICE CONTROLS DO". Supply/demand diagram: equilibrium price above the ceiling; shaded gap labelled "SHORTAGE". Then a second channel: "FUTURES MARKET — supplier of last resort".

Economics offers a clear prediction about prices held artificially low: they create shortages. Plywood became scarce. And no one was certain whether the futures market was bound by the same ceiling. {C18}

Then Marcus watched the futures price touch one hundred and ten dollars… and keep going. A hundred and ten, ten. A hundred and ten, twenty. Nobody seemed to know what would happen. The next day, it opened higher still. {C18}

His reasoning was simple: if they let it trade above the ceiling today, they might let it trade anywhere. He bought one contract. {C18}

[VISUAL] Chart: plywood futures rising from $110 to ~$200 (stylised from Marcus's figures). Pyramid blocks stack as price rises; each new block smaller than the one below.

As Marcus told it, plywood eventually went to around two hundred dollars. As the price rose, he added to his position — a technique called pyramiding. {C18}

Pyramiding means using profits from a winning position to add more, ideally in smaller amounts each time, so the position grows only while the market proves you right. It's how a small account becomes a large one. But it carries a risk: if you add too much, too late, a reversal can wipe out everything you've built.

In a few months, the seven hundred dollars had grown to about twelve thousand. {C18}

[VISUAL] Context card: "Futures as a two-tier market — buyers frozen out of the regulated cash market could still buy, at a higher price, in futures" (interpretation of Marcus's account).

The futures market had become, in effect, a supplier of last resort. Buyers who couldn't get plywood through normal channels could buy it there, at a higher price. Marcus saw an inefficiency created by government policy — and the confusion that surrounded it — and recognised it before most others did.

## 06 · CHAPTER FIVE — THE SAME MISTAKE TWICE

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "CHAPTER FIVE · The Same Mistake Twice".

And then he did it again.

He reasoned that lumber, also a wood product and also in short supply, should follow plywood through its ceiling. It was a logical idea. He bet everything on it. {C19}

He bought lumber at about a hundred and thirty dollars. The very next day, as he remembered it, a government official announced a crackdown on speculators trying to repeat the plywood run in lumber. The market crashed on the statement. For two weeks, more official warnings followed. {C19}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "WHY A SMALL MOVE CAN DESTROY A BIG POSITION". Left: plywood +82% move, small position. Right: lumber −10% move, large position. Account bar shrinks $12,000 → under $4,000. Callout: "≈ 6–7× exposure" (our calculation).

Lumber fell to around a hundred and seventeen — a decline of about ten percent. Yet Marcus's twelve thousand dollars fell below four thousand. {C19}

Do the arithmetic. A ten percent fall in price cost him about two-thirds of his account. That implies his market exposure was six to seven times his capital. This is the hidden danger of leverage: it isn't the size of the market move that destroys traders. It's the size of the position. {A02}

Those two weeks, he said, were the worst of his life. His hands shook. Each day he went to the office ready to give up. {C19}

The market stabilised just above the level that would have wiped him out. He held on — out of desperation more than courage, he admitted, though he noticed a support level on the chart that prices wouldn't break. Eventually lumber turned. By the end of 1972, the account was worth about twenty-four thousand dollars. {C19}{C20}

But the real gain was a rule. After lumber, Marcus said, he never again bet everything on one trade. {C20}

[VISUAL] Lesson card: "Survive first. Profit second."

## 07 · CHAPTER SIX — THE GREAT INFLATION

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "CHAPTER SIX · The Great Inflation". Montage of rising commodity lines (stylised).

In 1973, the price controls began to loosen. After two years of artificially suppressed prices and shortages, many commodities exploded upward. Prices doubled in market after market. {C21}{S12}

Marcus was positioned for it — and so was his worldview. He described himself at the time as politically right-wing and an "inflation alarmist," convinced governments were constantly debasing their currencies. It was, as Schwager put it, the right theory for the right time. Marcus agreed: markets were so fertile that he could make plenty of mistakes and still do well. In 1973, his account grew from twenty-four to sixty-four thousand dollars. {C21}

[VISUAL] Soybean chart: ~$3.25 rising to a peak; data card "Soybean futures record ≈ $12.90 · June 1973". Source: Univ. of Illinois farmdoc. A gold line marks "Marcus exits" early in the rise.

But his most painful moment of that year was not a loss. It was a win he let go.

During the great soybean bull market, he impulsively took his profits and stepped aside. Seykota, who never exited unless the trend changed, stayed in. Marcus watched soybeans go limit-up day after day — twelve days in a row, as he remembered it — with no way to get back in. Every morning he dreaded going to work, knowing Seykota was in and he was out. {C22}

The anguish was worse than losing money. He tried tranquillisers. Then someone suggested a much stronger drug. He took it, collapsed on the way to work, and missed the day. He called it the low point of his trading career. {C23}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "WHY WINNERS MATTER MORE THAN YOU THINK". Bar chart of 20 hypothetical trades: 14 small losses, 4 small wins, 2 large wins. Remove the 2 large wins → total turns negative.

Schwager drew a striking conclusion from this: Marcus's most devastating experience was a profitable trade. For a trend trader, a handful of big winners pays for everything else. Exit them too early, and the whole system breaks.

Shortly afterwards, the situation reversed. Seykota recommended buying coffee. Marcus bought, but placed a close stop. The market turned down; Marcus was stopped out with a small loss. Seykota, with no stop, was locked in a falling market for several limit-down days. {C24}

Marcus confessed that he felt a flash of joy. And then a question: what kind of place was this, where his greatest happiness came from someone else's pain? He decided the competitiveness of the desk was poisoning him. He would become a floor trader. {C24}

## 08 · CHAPTER SEVEN — THE FLOOR

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "CHAPTER SEVEN · The Floor". Stylised trading ring, concentric steps, raised hands.

The New York Cotton Exchange required a net worth of a hundred thousand dollars. After several more profitable months, Marcus qualified. {C25}

It went badly. He was good at picking trades, he said, but hopeless at the shouting. Too shy to make himself heard in the ring, he ended up quietly passing his orders to a friend to execute. {C25}

Yet he later recommended the experience to anyone who wanted to become a better trader.

[VISUAL] Explainer — "READING THE ROOM". Sound waveform rising and falling beneath a price line. Annotations: "loud → quiet: move exhausting", "moderate → very loud: hitting opposing orders".

On the floor, he said, you develop an almost subconscious feel for the market. You learn to read it through the intensity of voices. When an active market suddenly goes quiet, the move is often running out. When a moderately loud ring suddenly becomes very loud, it may not be about to explode — it may be colliding with a wall of opposing orders. {C26}

The most valuable thing he took away was respect for intraday chart points — like the earlier highs of the day. {C26}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "THE SURFING TECHNIQUE". Intraday chart; prior high marked. Large position entered at the break; stop 10 points below; target 300–400 points. Risk/reward bar: 1R vs 30–40R. Then hypothetical table: "Win 1 in 10 at 30R, lose 9 at 1R → net +21R" (labelled hypothetical).

At such a point, Marcus would take a position far larger than he could normally afford — twenty contracts instead of three to five — with an extremely close stop. Either the market took off, or he was out almost immediately. He might risk ten points to make three or four hundred. He compared it to surfing: catch the crest at exactly the right moment, or bail out. {C26}

Look at the mathematics. If you risk one unit to make thirty, you can be wrong nine times out of ten and still come out well ahead. That's what asymmetric risk means. {A04}

But Marcus added a warning. By 1988 he believed the technique no longer worked as well. Markets had become choppier; a break of an intraday high was more likely to snap back. {C26}

## 09 · CHAPTER EIGHT — "HE JUST TRADES"

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "CHAPTER EIGHT · He Just Trades". Data card: "Commodities Corporation · Princeton · founded 1969 by Helmut Weymar · backers included Paul Samuelson and Amos Hostetter". Source: Goldman Sachs history.

His escape from the floor came through his old mentor, Amos Hostetter, who was also trading for a firm in Princeton called Commodities Corporation. {C27}

Founded in 1969 by the economist Helmut Weymar, the firm had prestigious backers, including the Nobel laureate Paul Samuelson. Its founding idea was to hire brilliant econometricians and turn them into traders. {C27}

When Marcus's name came up, the first question, as he remembered it, was what he had published, and in which journals. The answer was: nothing. He had a bachelor's degree. The punchline — "He just trades" — drew laughter. Hostetter persuaded them anyway. Marcus believed he was the first trader they ever hired without a PhD. {C28}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "THE POWER OF COMPOUNDING". Gold curve from $30,000 (Aug 1974) to $80 million (~10 years). Overlay: "30% annual levy taken by the firm". Callout: "2,500× in 10 years ≈ 119% per year, compounded" (our calculation).

In August 1974 they gave him thirty thousand dollars. After a few years, a further hundred thousand. From then on, the firm mostly took money out — charging its traders around thirty percent a year to cover its expenses. After about ten years, Marcus said, the account stood at eighty million dollars. {C29}

Schwager describes it as a twenty-five-hundred-fold increase. To put that in perspective: multiplying money twenty-five hundred times in ten years requires an average compound return of roughly a hundred and nineteen percent a year — every year, for a decade. Marcus himself said he made at least a hundred percent a year, for years and years. {C30}{C31}{A01}

[VISUAL] Disclosure card: "Performance figures as reported by Marcus and Schwager; no independent audited record is publicly available."

It is important to be clear: these figures come from Marcus and Schwager. No independent audited record is publicly available. But Schwager knew Marcus personally, and the firm's later history speaks for itself: in 1997, Goldman Sachs acquired Commodities Corporation. {S04}

For almost two years Marcus traded little except cocoa. Weymar was one of the world's leading cocoa experts, with a network of contacts across the industry. Marcus said he came to know the universe of cocoa as he had never known any market — and, he added drily, he traded Weymar's information better than Weymar did. {C32}

## 10 · CHAPTER NINE — GOLD, CURRENCIES AND THE NIGHT SHIFT

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "CHAPTER NINE · The Night Shift". World map at night; market opening times light up in sequence: Sydney, Hong Kong, Zurich, London, New York.

By the late 1970s Marcus had moved to California — which, he discovered, gave him an advantage. While his New York colleagues slept, he could trade in Hong Kong. {C35}

[VISUAL] Timeline: "Late 1978 — dollar falling to new lows" → "a sudden, unexplained rally" → "Marcus & Kovner exit" → "Carter dollar-rescue program, 1 Nov 1978". Source: American Presidency Project.

In late 1978, the dollar was being battered, falling to new lows. Marcus was trading closely with a protégé, Bruce Kovner — they spoke for hours every day. One day they noticed the dollar suddenly strengthening for no reason they could find. They sold their long foreign-currency positions immediately. {C33}

Soon after, President Carter announced a major program to defend the dollar. Currency futures collapsed for several days. Marcus and Kovner were already out. {C33}

[VISUAL] Fact-check note on screen: "Marcus recalled the announcement as 'that weekend'; the official announcement was on Wednesday, 1 November 1978."

A note for accuracy: Marcus remembered the announcement coming that weekend. The official record dates it to Wednesday, the first of November, 1978. The details of memory fade; the principle he drew from it did not. {C34}

That principle: the big players — including governments — tend to tip their hand. When a market moves sharply against you and you can't explain why, get out first, and look for the reason later. {C33}

[VISUAL] Gold chart 1979–1980 (stylised): rise to ~$850 peak (data card: "Gold peak ≈ $850 · 21 Jan 1980" — source: Bloomberg). Marker: "Marcus exit ≈ $750".

1979, he said, was his best year. Gold was in a near-vertical bull market. He would buy in Australia, watch Hong Kong push it ten dollars higher and London another ten, and sell in New York at a thirty-dollar profit. {C31}

Then came the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan. Marcus saw the news on television and called Hong Kong. Nobody there seemed to know; the price hadn't moved. By his account, he bought two hundred thousand ounces of gold before the news spread. When it did, minutes later, he had an immediate profit of about ten dollars an ounce — on his own figures, roughly two million dollars. He said it was the first and only time he ever traded off television news. He was later told not to visit the Hong Kong gold floor. {C35}{A05}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "THE LIMIT-DAY EXIT RULE". Calendar strip of consecutive limit-up days: Day 3 amber "Caution", Day 4 orange "Usually out", Day 5 red "Mandatory exit".

He sold at around seven hundred and fifty dollars. Gold went on to about eight hundred and fifty in January 1980 — Marcus remembered it as nearly nine hundred. He felt sick, he said. But when gold later fell back toward four hundred, he felt much better. {C36}

How did he know to leave? He had a rule for wild markets. When a market went limit-up three days in a row, he became very cautious. On the fourth, he almost always got out. And if anything remained by the fifth, he had a mandatory rule to exit. Insane volatility and momentum, he believed, signalled the end of a move, not the beginning. {C37}

[VISUAL] Data card: "Up to 600 million Deutsche marks ≈ $300 million (Marcus's account)". Clock graphic waking every two hours.

In the early 1980s, by his account, Marcus held currency positions as large as six hundred million Deutsche marks — about three hundred million dollars — across his own and the company account. He believed he was among the larger currency traders in the world. The market never closed, so he woke almost every two hours to check Australia, Hong Kong, Zurich and London. It ended his marriage, he said. {C38}

And size brought its own danger. Once, the German central bank intervened to punish speculators. Marcus was down two and a half million dollars in about five minutes. He got out — rather than risk ten million. Half an hour later, the market had recovered its entire fall. {C39}

He still believed he did the right thing. A stop protects you from the disaster you can't foresee — even if, this time, the disaster didn't arrive.

## 11 · CHAPTER TEN — MARKET TONE

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "CHAPTER TEN · Market Tone".

If there is one idea at the heart of Marcus's mature method, it is this. The best trades have three things going for them at once. {M01}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "THE THREE CONDITIONS". Three pillars light up in sequence: FUNDAMENTALS ("supply/demand imbalance"), TECHNICALS ("chart moving the same way"), MARKET TONE ("reaction to news confirms"). A gold capstone appears only when all three are lit: "A-GRADE TRADE · 5–6× normal size".

First, fundamentals: an imbalance of supply and demand that could drive a major move. Second, technicals: the chart must show the market already moving in that direction. Third, market tone: when news arrives, the market must react the right way. A bull market should shrug off bad news and leap on good news. {M01}

Restrict yourself to those trades, Marcus said, and you must make money — in any market, under any circumstances. {M01}

Then he confessed something unusual. He didn't follow his own advice. He enjoyed the game too much; trading was his hobby and his release. What saved him was sizing. When a trade met all three criteria, he put on five or six times his normal size. All his profits, he said, came from those trades. The rest roughly broke even — and kept him amused. {M02}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "WHEN GOOD NEWS FAILS". Soybean price: opens at limit-up, ticks, slips below limit. Marcus's order reverses: SELL. Short position profits 40–50¢.

His favourite example came from a late-1970s soybean bull market. Marcus was heavily long. A colleague called with spectacular export figures: the market was expected to go limit-up for three days. He even added to his position at the next opening. {M09}

Soybeans opened limit-up — and then slipped off the limit. If soybeans were supposed to be limit-up for three days, and couldn't hold it for one morning, something was badly wrong. He sold frantically — so frantically that he lost count, and ended up substantially short. He bought it back forty to fifty cents lower: the only time, he said, he made a lot of money on an error. {M09}

[VISUAL] Lesson card: "When the news is wonderful and the market can't go up — sell."

The rule is one of the most powerful in trading. Prices reflect expectations. When the best possible news fails to move a market, it means everyone who wanted to buy has already bought.

[VISUAL] Explainer — "WHO IS LEFT TO BUY?". 1970s chain: professionals → grain elevator → brokerage clients → short covering → "the dentists" (latecomers). 1980s: all participants act at once; chain collapses into a single spike; false breakout.

Marcus applied the same thinking to how markets were changing. In the early 1970s, he said, there was a slow chain of buyers after a breakout: professionals first, then commercial users, then brokerage clients, then — in his words — "the dentists of the world," who arrived last. By the late 1980s, the moment a market broke a key level, everyone saw it at once. The follow-through buyers were gone. Breakouts failed more often. {M08}{M15}

So the trader must always ask: how many people are left to act on this idea? Has the market already priced it in? {M08}

## 12 · CHAPTER ELEVEN — THE PRICE OF SUCCESS

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "CHAPTER ELEVEN · The Price of Success". Icons: houses, planes, staff — each draining coins.

For all his skill in markets, Marcus was candid about his failures outside them. He owned around ten houses — some he sold without spending a night in them — ran a plane charter service, and employed sixty or seventy people. Of every dollar he made trading, he once estimated, thirty cents went to tax, thirty to the planes, and twenty to the real estate. {C40}

[VISUAL] Stacked bar: $1 → 30% tax · 30% planes · 20% real estate · 20% left.

He said he probably lost more than half of everything he made — and that when staff warned him, he fired them. He invested emotionally, without analysis: repeating, outside the markets, the very mistake of his first trades. {C40}

He took it, he said, as a life lesson. He didn't need to own a house in every beautiful place in the world; he could stay at a hotel and walk on the beach.

## 13 · CHAPTER TWELVE — THE RULES

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "CHAPTER TWELVE · The Rules". Each rule appears as a numbered card in gold.

When Schwager asked what he would tell a beginning or losing trader, Marcus did not hesitate.

**Rule one: risk less than five percent of your money on any one idea.** {M03}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "5% PER IDEA". Equity bar shrinking by 5% per loss over 20 losses: 100 → 36. Second panel: two grain positions grouped under one bracket: "ONE IDEA".

That way, he said, you can be wrong more than twenty times before you are wiped out. In fact, losing five percent twenty times in a row would still leave about a third of the account. And note his precise wording: one *idea*. Buying two related grain markets is still one idea. If both fall together, they are one bet. {M03}{A03}

**Rule two: always use stops — and decide your exit before you enter.** Marcus said he always picked his exit point before taking a trade. A stop order commits you. {M04}

**Rule three: if it doesn't feel right, get out.** Don't worry about what your broker will think. When in doubt, get out, and get a good night's sleep. While you're in a position, he said, you can't think clearly. Once you're out, you can. Often he went back in the next day. {M05}

**Rule four: hold your winners and cut your losers. Both are equally important.** In his words, "If you don't stay with your winners, you are not going to be able to pay for the losers." {M06}

**Rule five: follow your own light.** Marcus had many brilliant friends. But when he traded their ideas, he lost. A trade that isn't yours gives you none of the conviction needed to hold it — and mixing styles, he said, often gives you the worst of both. {M07}

**Rule six: watch your equity curve.** If it trends down, cut back and reassess. In a losing streak, his instinct was to fight harder — and it rarely worked. Losing, he believed, begets losing: it feeds pessimism. So he cut size quickly, and sometimes stopped completely for three or four weeks. {M10}

[VISUAL] Equity curve with a declining section highlighted; position-size bars shrinking beneath it.

**Rule seven: don't rely on experts.** Most so-called experts, he said, are not traders. Trading requires intense personal involvement. Do your own homework. And don't believe the market is controlled by conspiracies: almost always, the market is bigger than anyone, and it goes where it wants to go. {M11}

Beyond the rules, he described the qualities that cannot be written down. Gut feel. And courage — the courage to try, to fail, to succeed, and to keep going when things are hard. To reach the very top, he believed, requires a gift, like a great violinist. But becoming a competent, profitable trader is a skill that can be learned. {M12}

[VISUAL] Profile card: "BRUCE KOVNER — founded Caxton Associates, 1983". Source: Wikipedia.

The quality he valued most in others was open-mindedness. When he met Bruce Kovner — then a writer and academic who traded on the side — what struck him was Kovner's objectivity. A good trader, Marcus said, cannot be rigid. Kovner went on to found Caxton Associates in 1983 and became one of the most successful macro traders of his era. {C41}{C42}

## 14 · CHAPTER THIRTEEN — WAS HE RIGHT?

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "CHAPTER THIRTEEN · Was He Right?". Analysis segment — on-screen label: "ANALYSIS · OUR INTERPRETATION".

In May 1988, Marcus made a bold claim. The era of trend following, he said, was over — unless a powerful imbalance overwhelmed everything else, such as a drought, or a major bout of inflation or deflation. He pointed to Richard Dennis, one of the most famous trend followers, whose public funds had suffered heavy losses. {M15}

[VISUAL] Data card: "1987–88 — several Richard Dennis public funds stopped trading after losses exceeding 50%." Source: Wikipedia/Futures (1988). Data card: "1988 drought — US corn output −37%, soybeans −23%." Source: Chicago Fed.

Part of that is verifiable. Several of Dennis's public funds did stop trading after losses beyond fifty percent in 1987 and 1988. And within weeks of the interview, Marcus's own exception arrived: the great drought of 1988 cut American corn production by more than a third, and grain prices surged. {S14}{S17}

Was the larger claim right? Here we offer interpretation, not fact. Trend following did not disappear. Systematic trend-following funds continued to operate for decades and enjoyed powerful years when big, persistent moves appeared. But Marcus's deeper point has aged well: as more participants see the same signals at the same time, simple breakouts become less reliable, and edges that once came from speed or information get competed away. {A06}

[VISUAL] Two-column card. STRENGTHS: asymmetric payoffs · strict loss limits · tiered position sizing · adaptability · brutal self-honesty. WEAKNESSES: impulsiveness · trading for entertainment · reliance on a uniquely favourable 1970s regime · lifestyle leakage · burnout.

Marcus's approach had clear strengths: small, defined losses; large winners; size concentrated on the best setups; and a rare willingness to admit mistakes. It also had weaknesses, many of which he named himself: impulsiveness, trading for entertainment, a career launched in what he called an extraordinarily favourable market, and a personal life that leaked away much of what the markets gave him.

What remains relevant today? Almost all of the rules. Position sizing. Stops decided before entry. Letting winners run. Watching how a market reacts to news. Asking who is left to buy. These are not features of 1970s commodity markets. They are features of human behaviour, and that, Marcus believed, never changes. Trading, he said, is emotion — mass psychology, greed and fear — the same in every market.

## 15 · EPILOGUE

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "EPILOGUE". Slow fade to warm charcoal. Data card: "Michael Marcus · 1947 – 2023". Source: Wikipedia; Jack Schwager (2023).

Asked whether he would still be trading in ten or twenty years, Marcus said yes — it was too much fun to give up. But he added a condition. Trading as your whole life, he said, is a torturous kind of excitement. Traders who last find balance; they have something outside the markets. {M13}

He spoke about studying karate, and about spiritual traditions. He quoted Albert Einstein's question — whether the universe is friendly — and said he had started his life feeling that it wasn't. Now, he said, he was a lot closer.

Michael Marcus died in March 2023, in Austin, Texas. When Jack Schwager learned of it, he described Marcus as one of the greatest traders he ever knew — and called his brutally honest interview, the first chapter of the first *Market Wizards* book, his favourite. {S02}{S03}

[VISUAL] Return to the image from the cold open: the frozen corn board. It unfreezes; prices move again.

He began with eight straight losses. He was wiped out, then nearly wiped out again. He missed the trade of a lifetime, and once made a fortune by mistake.

What made him a Market Wizard was not that he avoided failure. It was that, each time, he turned it into a rule.
