## The 19 dimensions
1. **Biography & background** — middle-class family; father died when Marcus was 15 (C07). Scholar by temperament.
2. **Early life & education** — Johns Hopkins 1969; psychology fellowship at Clark (C02–C03). The psychology background echoes through his view that "trading is emotion."
3. **Entry into markets** — naive promise of "doubling money every two weeks"; a hired student adviser; eight straight losers (C04–C05).
4. **Career development** — research analyst → secret trader → Seykota/Hostetter apprenticeship → plywood breakthrough → floor → Commodities Corporation → among the largest currency traders of the early 1980s (by his account).
5. **Philosophy** — markets are mass psychology; principles transfer across all markets; follow your own light.
6. **Methodology** — discretionary trend riding (learned from Seykota) combined with fundamentals and "market tone"; tiered sizing (M01–M02).
7. **Technical & fundamental** — fundamentals identify imbalance; chart must confirm; news reaction tests the psychology (M01). Intraday chart points from floor experience (C26).
8. **Markets & instruments** — grains, wood products, softs (cocoa, coffee, cotton), metals (gold, silver), currencies, later small-cap stocks (M14).
9. **Entry & exit** — breakouts at key chart points; exit on adverse unexplained moves; limit-day exhaustion rule (C37); exit when confused (M05).
10. **Risk management** — stops always, exit chosen before entry (M04); act on suspicious price action before knowing why (C33).
11. **Position sizing** — ≤5% per idea; correlated = one idea (M03); 5–6× on A-grade setups (M02); "surfing" with tiny risk and large size (C26).
12. **Capital preservation** — two near-ruins taught "never bet everything" (C13, C19–C20); cut size in drawdowns (M10).
13. **Psychology** — paralysis in the corn crash; manic secrecy at Reynolds; the anguish of missing a winner exceeded losing (C22–C23); competitiveness pushed him off the desk and onto the floor (C24).
14. **Discipline & decision-making** — confesses he traded for fun outside his criteria; saved by sizing (M02). Open-mindedness as core edge.
15. **Successes & failures** — plywood, 1973 boom, cocoa, 1978 currency exit, 1979 gold; failures: corn 1971, lumber 1972, soybean early exit, Bundesbank, real estate & lifestyle losses (C40).
16. **Historical market experiences** — corn blight; Nixon controls; 1973 inflation boom; dollar crisis 1978; gold mania 1979–80; central-bank interventions.
17. **Lessons** — see Chapter 12 of outline.
18. **Strengths & weaknesses** — Strengths: asymmetric payoff structure, adaptive, recognised changing market structure early, honest self-assessment. Weaknesses: impulsiveness, overtrading for entertainment, heavy reliance on an exceptionally favourable 1970s regime (he says so himself), lifestyle leakage, burnout risk (24-hour currency trading).
19. **Relevance today** — sizing and stop discipline are universal; "who is left to buy?" is more relevant in crowded, algorithmic markets; intraday "surfing" edge likely arbitraged (his own view, 1988); central-bank-driven markets echo his currency caution. His 1988 call that trend following was "over" is debatable (A06) — present as a view, with the counter-evidence of the 1988 drought.
