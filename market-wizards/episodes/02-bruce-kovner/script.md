## 01 · COLD OPEN

[VISUAL] Black. A quote board: SOYBEANS JULY and NOVEMBER, both rising. A telephone handset icon lights up. Slow push-in.

April the thirteenth, 1977. Soybean prices are climbing so fast that the whole market is in uproar. A young trader's phone rings at home. It is his broker. {C17}{C18}

Soybeans, the broker says, are going to the moon. You're a fool to stay short the November contracts. Let me lift them for you. {C18}

The trader holds a carefully balanced position: long one soybean contract, short another. The short side is his protection. He says yes. All of it. {C18}

[VISUAL] Board flips: both rows turn red. "LIMIT DOWN". Clock: "+15 minutes".

Fifteen minutes later, the broker calls back, sounding frantic. The market is limit-down. He doesn't know if he can get him out. {C18}

By the end of the day, an account that had been worth about forty-five thousand dollars that morning held twenty-two thousand. He called it a moment of insanity. {C19}

[VISUAL] Title card sequence: name fades in over the frozen board.

His name was Bruce Kovner. Ten years later, he would be among the largest traders in the world's currency and futures markets. According to Jack Schwager, Kovner's average annual compounded return over that decade was eighty-seven percent. Two thousand dollars invested with him in early 1978 would have grown to more than a million. {C28}

He would build that record on one idea above all others: never again be caught in a position that can hurt you badly for reasons you don't understand. {M12}

This is the story of how a political scientist learned to read the world — and to survive being wrong about it.

## 02 · CHAPTER ONE — THE PROFESSOR

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "CHAPTER ONE · The Professor". Ledger: born 1945, New York · Harvard · political science.

Bruce Kovner was born in New York in 1945. When he was a boy, his family moved from Brooklyn to the suburbs of Los Angeles. He went east again for college — to Harvard. {C01}{C02}{C03}

He stayed in academia. He studied political economy, completed the coursework for a doctorate, and taught political science at Harvard and at the University of Pennsylvania. Later profiles say he never finished the degree. {C03}{C04}

He liked teaching. What he didn't like, he told Schwager, was the writing — confronting a blank page every morning and trying to think of something brilliant to say. {C05}

[VISUAL] Timeline strip: "Harvard → teaching → political campaigns → consultant → markets (mid-1970s)".

In the early 1970s he tried politics instead. He managed several political campaigns, with the idea of one day running for office himself. He gave it up, Schwager writes, because he didn't have the money, or the desire to climb the ladder one committee job at a time. He worked as a consultant to state and federal agencies. {C06}

He was still searching for a direction when, in the mid-1970s, he turned to the financial markets. He believed his training in economics and political science was the right background. Analysing the whole world in order to make trading decisions struck him as tremendously appealing. For about a year, he read everything he could find. {C07}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "THE YIELD CURVE". Axes: maturity (3 months → 10 years) vs yield. A rising curve draws itself; dots at "1-year T-bill" and "5-year T-note". Label: "normal (upward-sloping) curve".

One subject captured him completely. As he put it, he "fell in love with the yield curve." {C08}

The yield curve is simply a picture. Plot what the government pays to borrow for three months, for one year, for five years, for ten. Usually, the longer you lend, the more you are paid, so the line slopes upward. But its shape changes as the economy moves through its cycle, and every point on it carries a forecast about interest rates in the future. For a mind that liked hidden structure, it was irresistible.

## 03 · CHAPTER TWO — THE ANOMALY

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "CHAPTER TWO · The Anomaly". Data card: "Oct 1975 — first interest-rate futures (GNMA, Chicago Board of Trade) · Jan 1976 — Treasury-bill futures open on the IMM". Source: Richmond Fed; CFTC.

Kovner arrived at exactly the right moment. Interest-rate futures were brand new. The first contract opened in Chicago in October 1975; Treasury-bill futures followed in January 1976. {C09}

New markets are often inefficient. Price distortions that professionals would later wipe out in seconds could last for weeks. In Kovner's words, the market hadn't yet become important enough for the giant banks — "but it was important enough for me." {C10}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "A CALENDAR SPREAD". Four contract months on a strip: MAR · JUN · SEP · DEC. Theory line shows each nearer month slightly above the next. Reality: the two most distant months priced almost the same — gap highlighted "≈ 0". Arrow: "Buy the nearer of the two, sell the more distant — and wait".

Here is what he found. Futures trade for specific delivery months. Given the state of the business cycle, theory said each nearer contract should trade a little above the next one out. The two nearest contracts did behave that way. But further along the strip, contracts traded at almost no difference at all. {C11}

So he placed his first trade. He bought one of the more distant contracts and sold one further out still. As time passed, the contract he owned would become a nearer contract — and if the theory was right, the gap between the two would open up. {C11}

It worked just as the textbook said it would. Kovner, Schwager writes, was hooked. {C11}

His second trade used the same structure in copper. He expected tight supplies to lift the nearby contract relative to a later one. He bought the near month and sold the distant one. His idea, he said, eventually proved right. But he was too early, and lost money. {C12}

[VISUAL] Ledger: "Starting stake $3,000 → after two trades ≈ $4,000".

After those two trades, his original three thousand dollars had become about four thousand. A modest start — and a first lesson in the difference between being right and being right on time. {C12}

[VISUAL] Small flagged card: "Often repeated, not verified: 'borrowed on a credit card'".

Later profiles often add that the three thousand dollars was borrowed on a credit card. We could not trace that detail to any primary source, so treat it as legend, not fact. {C55}

## 04 · CHAPTER THREE — THE SPREAD

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "CHAPTER THREE · The Spread". Data card: "Spring 1977 — U.S. soybean supplies tight; analysts warn stocks must be rationed until harvest". Source: Univ. of Illinois farmdoc, Apr 1977.

His third trade, Kovner said, is what really put him in the business. {C13}

In early 1977, a soybean shortage seemed to be developing. Week after week, the crush — the amount of soybeans processed into meal and oil — came in higher than expected. Nobody believed the figures. Agricultural economists at the time warned that supplies were so tight that soybeans would have to be rationed until the autumn harvest. {C13}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "OLD CROP vs NEW CROP". Two bins: JULY (old crop — the beans already harvested) and NOVEMBER (new crop — still to be planted). If the old crop runs out, JULY rises faster: the "premium" grows. Gauge: July premium over November, centred at 60¢.

Kovner watched the spread between two contracts: July, the last of the old crop, and November, the new crop that hadn't even been planted. If the country really was running out of soybeans, the July contract should gain on November. {C14}

The spread had been resting in a narrow range, with July about sixty cents above November. He reasoned he could easily protect himself by getting out if it fell to around forty-five cents. He bought July and sold November near sixty cents. It widened to seventy. He added another. And another. He kept pyramiding. {C14}

[VISUAL] Pyramid of spread positions growing; counter "≈ 15 spreads". Margin card: "Spread margin $400 · Outright margin $2,000".

By the time he had about ten or fifteen spreads, the head of his small brokerage firm — an old floor trader — noticed. The margin on a spread was only four hundred dollars, against two thousand for an outright contract. The old trader told him his spread was really an outright long position in disguise, and raised his margin fivefold. {C15}{A04}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "WHEN A HEDGE ISN'T A HEDGE". Normal market: July and November move together; spread barely moves. Shortage: July rockets, November lags — spread swings as violently as a single outright position.

The old trader had a point. Usually, the two legs of a spread move together, so one cancels much of the other's risk. That is why exchanges charge less margin. But in a shortage, the old-crop contract can explode while the new crop barely moves. An intercrop spread can then become almost as volatile as a plain bet on rising prices. {C15}

Kovner admitted the man was right. He was furious anyway — he saw an obstacle to his objective — and moved his account to a major brokerage house, with a broker he later described as not very competent. {C15}

The market kept rising, and he kept adding. He had put on his first spread on the twenty-fifth of February. By the twelfth of April, the account was worth about thirty-five thousand dollars. {C16}

## 05 · CHAPTER FOUR — FIFTEEN MINUTES

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "CHAPTER FOUR · Fifteen Minutes". Date card: "13 APRIL 1977".

The next day, soybeans went into a string of limit-up moves. As Kovner remembered it, the market hit a new record high. {C17}

[VISUAL] Fact-check note on screen: "Kovner recalled 'a new record high'. The all-time high was ≈ $12.90 in June 1973; the 1977 peak was ≈ $10.50–10.77 in late April. Source: farmdoc."

A note for accuracy. The all-time high in soybeans had been set four years earlier, in June 1973, near twelve dollars and ninety cents. The 1977 rally peaked a little above ten and a half dollars later that April. Kovner may have meant a record for that contract or that season. The detail doesn't change the story. {C17}

The commotion was tremendous. His broker called him at home. July was going limit-up, the broker said, and November was sure to follow. Why stay short November? Lift the shorts, and when the market goes limit-up for the next few days, you'll make even more. {C18}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "WHAT ONE YES CHANGED". Before: LONG JULY + SHORT NOVEMBER (bracket "spread"). The SHORT NOVEMBER block slides away. After: LONG JULY alone — label "naked long, maximum leverage". Price drops; account bar falls $45,000 → $22,000. Callout: "−51% in one session (our calculation)".

Kovner agreed. They covered his entire November short position. It was, he said, a moment of insanity. {C18}

Think about what that one decision did. With the short side in place, a sudden fall in soybeans would hit both legs, and much of the loss would cancel out. With the short side gone, he was simply long — a huge number of contracts bought on spread margin, with nothing on the other side. {A04}

Fifteen minutes later, the broker called back. He didn't know how to say it, but the market was limit-down. He didn't know if he could get Kovner out. {C18}

Kovner went into shock and yelled at him to get out. The market lifted slightly off its limit, and he was out. At the moment he lifted the shorts, he was up about forty-five thousand dollars. By the end of the day, the account held twenty-two thousand. Roughly half his money, gone in a single session. {C19}{A02}

He was sick to his stomach, he said. He didn't eat for days. He thought he had blown his career as a trader. {C19}

Schwager pushed back. Kovner still had twenty-two thousand dollars — far more than he started with. Kovner agreed. It wasn't the money, he said. It was the realisation that there really was fire there. He had ridden three thousand dollars to forty-five thousand without a moment of pain. And it had been easy. {C19}{C20}

[VISUAL] Ledger: "$3,000 → ≈ $45,000 (peak) → $22,000 · 'nearly sixfold' (Kovner)". Note: "$22,000 ÷ $4,000 ≈ 5.5× — our calculation".

What hurt, he explained, was discovering that he had lost a process of rationality he thought he had. In the middle of a panic, he had removed his own protection on a phone call. Markets, he learned, could take money away just as fast as they gave it. {C20}

He called it his going-bust trade — the closest he ever came to going broke, and psychologically, it felt as if he had. It was his most painful trade by far. And yet, on paper, he had multiplied his money nearly sixfold. He had been, in his own words, insanely leveraged, without understanding how risky his position was. {C20}{A03}

[VISUAL] Lesson card: "Never be caught where you can lose a great deal — for reasons you don't understand."

After that day, the market went straight down as fast as it had gone up. The spread collapsed, eventually falling below the level where he first bought it. If he hadn't panicked out, he admitted, he might have made the bigger mistake of riding it all the way down. {C21}

The lesson stayed with him for life. To this day, he told Schwager, whenever something happens that disturbs his sense of what the world is like, he closes out all positions related to it. On the nineteenth and twentieth of October, 1987 — the week of the stock-market crash — he closed out everything, because something was happening in the world that he didn't understand. {C22}{M12}

## 06 · CHAPTER FIVE — THE INTERVIEW

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "CHAPTER FIVE · The Interview". Newspaper-style classified ad (stylised, not a reproduction): "TRADING ASSISTANT WANTED".

About a month later, Kovner was trading again. Within a few months, the account was back to around forty thousand dollars. Around then, he answered an advertisement for a trading assistant at a firm in Princeton: Commodities Corporation. {C23}

[VISUAL] Profile card: "MICHAEL MARCUS · Commodities Corporation · subject of Episode One".

If you watched our first episode, you know who interviewed him: Michael Marcus. As Kovner recalled it, Marcus interviewed him in his usual idiosyncratic manner, then called him back a few weeks later with good news and bad news. The bad news: they would not hire him as a trading assistant. The good news: they would hire him as a trader. {C23}{S21}

Marcus, for his part, told Schwager what impressed him about the young writer and academic: his open-mindedness and objectivity. {S21}

Commodities Corporation gave Kovner thirty-five thousand dollars to trade. The firm also let its traders run their own accounts at the same time — something he said he was very glad about. He and Marcus were, in his words, very aggressive traders. {C24}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "THE CHAIN OF JUDGMENTS". Path of decisions: "Best judgment → wrong → next best → wrong → third best → ✓ doubling your money". Each wrong step is a small, capped red block; the final step a large gold block.

What did Marcus teach him? First, that it could be done. He showed Kovner that you really could make a million dollars — that if you applied yourself, took a position and used discipline, great things could happen. {C25}

And second, something Kovner called absolutely critical: you have to be willing to make mistakes regularly. Make your best judgment. Be wrong. Make your next best judgment. Be wrong again. Make your third best judgment — and double your money. {C25}{M13}

The point is not that being wrong is good. It's that a trader who can't accept being wrong will freeze — or hold on — when the market disagrees. A trader who keeps each mistake small can afford to keep looking until the right idea arrives.

[VISUAL] Timeline: "1977 Commodities Corporation → late 1978 dollar exit with Marcus → 1983 founds Caxton". Source: Ep. 01 research; Wikipedia — Caxton Associates.

Kovner thrived there. In late 1978, as we saw in Episode One, he and Marcus sold their foreign-currency positions just before the American government stepped in to defend the dollar. In 1983, Kovner left to found his own firm: Caxton. {C27}{S21}

## 07 · CHAPTER SIX — IMAGINING WORLDS

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "CHAPTER SIX · Imagining Worlds".

Schwager asked him the obvious question. There are only a handful of traders of your calibre. What makes you different? {M01}

Kovner was careful. He wasn't sure anyone could really define it. But for himself, he named two things. {M01}

[VISUAL] Two pillars: "1 · IMAGINATION — picture a world different from today, and believe it can happen" · "2 · DISCIPLINE — stay rational under pressure". Examples under pillar 1: "Soybeans double" · "Dollar falls to 100 yen".

First, the ability to imagine configurations of the world quite different from today — and to really believe they can happen. He could imagine soybean prices doubling, he said, or the dollar falling to a hundred yen. Second, the ability to stay rational and disciplined under pressure. {M01}

Notice how the two depend on each other. Imagination without discipline is how a trader blows up chasing a vision. Discipline without imagination is how a trader stays safe and never catches the big move.

[VISUAL] Explainer — "THE SCENARIO BOARD". A grid of world "pictures" (cards), each with ten small element dots. Most cards light only two or three dots. One card lights nine of ten — it lifts forward: "WORKING VIEW". Caption: "Assume today's price is right. Ask what could change it."

His method begins with a surprising assumption. He assumes that the price of a market on any given day is the correct price. His job is to work out what changes are happening that will alter it. {M02}

So he imagines alternatives. He forms many different mental pictures of what the world could look like, and tries them on one at a time. Inevitably, most turn out to be wrong — only a few elements of each prove correct. Then, suddenly, in one picture, nine out of ten elements click. That scenario becomes his image of the world. {M02}

[VISUAL] Chart (stylised): Canadian dollar range, then an upside breakout days before a marker "U.S.–Canada trade agreement reached · 3–4 Oct 1987". Source: CBC; Reagan Library.

He gave an example. For six months, he had good arguments for the Canadian dollar going down — and good arguments for it going up. If forced to choose, he would probably have said down. Then the United States and Canada announced a free-trade agreement. The deal was reached in early October 1987. {C34}

The market, he said, had already broken out on the upside a few days earlier, as the negotiations were finishing. To Kovner, that settled it. A major piece of the Canadian dollar's valuation had just changed — and the market had already voted. {C34}{M04}

Schwager challenged him. Wasn't it obvious the pact would be good for Canada? Kovner said no. He could just as easily have argued the opposite: that removing trade barriers would let American imports swamp Canadian industry. Some analysts still argued exactly that. {C34}

His point was humility. There are well-informed traders who know much more than he does, he said. He simply puts things together. When an important change occurs, the first direction of the market is often a good tip-off, because the market usually leads — there are people who know more than you. {M04}

He even named one of them. In currencies, and in grains to some degree, he considered the Soviet Union a very good trader. You could hear about its activity, he said, because it acted through commercial banks and dealers. Whatever one makes of that claim, the logic is the same: thousands of hard-to-see mechanisms move markets before the news reaches the average trader — but a large purchase or sale always shows up in the price. {M04}

## 08 · CHAPTER SEVEN — THE THERMOMETER

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "CHAPTER SEVEN · The Thermometer". A thermometer graphic beside a price chart: the mercury rises as volatility rises.

That leads to his view of charts. Technical analysis, Kovner said, has a great deal that is right — and a great deal that is "mumbo jumbo." {M05}

The hype comes from technicians who claim charts predict the future. They don't, he said. Technical analysis tracks the past. You have to use your own intelligence to judge what the past behaviour of some traders says about the future behaviour of others. {M05}

For him, it is a thermometer. A fundamentalist who ignores charts, he said, is like a doctor who refuses to take a patient's temperature. You always want to know where the market is — hot and excitable, or cold and stagnant. Charts reflect the vote of the entire marketplace, and anything that creates a new chart pattern is, by definition, something unusual. {M05}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "TWO KINDS OF BREAKOUT". Left: a tight, quiet range; price breaks up with no headline — label "Nobody knows why → strong". Right: a range breaking on a newspaper headline "CORN SHORTAGE FEARED" (stylised) — label "Everybody knows why → weak". Both hypothetical.

So yes, he will buy a breakout. But which kind? Tight congestions, he said, that break out for reasons nobody understands are usually good risk-reward trades. {M06}

And breakouts that happen because a story appears in the newspaper that morning? Much less relevant. He borrowed an idea from physics: the Heisenberg principle, loosely put, that observing something alters it. If corn breaks out of a quiet range on the day a famous newspaper reports a possible shortage, the move is less likely to last. If everyone believes corn has no reason to rise — and it suddenly does — there is far more likely to be an important cause underneath. {M06}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "WHEN EVERYONE SEES THE SAME SIGNAL". Many identical robot icons ("system money") all firing BUY at one line; price spikes then falls back: "false signal". Caption: "The more a pattern is watched, the less it is worth."

The same logic made him wary of crowded patterns. The more a pattern is watched by speculators, he said, the more prone it is to false signals. By 1988, billions of dollars were following computerised trend-following systems using moving averages and other simple rules. That money, he believed, produced many more false breakouts. {M07}

So he built similar systems himself — not to follow them blindly, but to know when they would kick in. If prices are moving only because those billions are buying, he said, the move is far less interesting than a breakout that happens because, say, the Russians are buying. {M07}

## 09 · CHAPTER EIGHT — THE STOP

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "CHAPTER EIGHT · The Stop".

Schwager asked the question every trader faces. You buy a breakout, and the price slips back into the range. How do you tell a small pullback from a bad trade? {M08}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "THE STOP COMES FIRST". Range drawn with support and resistance; entry at the breakout; stop placed beyond the far side of a technical barrier, not inside the range. Label sequence: "1. Decide the stop · 2. Measure the distance · 3. Size the position".

His answer was immediate. Whenever he enters a position, he has a predetermined stop. That, he said, is the only way he can sleep. He knows where he is getting out before he gets in. {M08}

Then comes the part most traders get backwards. The position size is determined by the stop — and the stop is determined on a technical basis. If a market is in the middle of a trading range, it makes no sense to put your stop inside that range, where ordinary noise will take you out. He places his stop beyond some technical barrier. {M08}

What about other traders hunting for the same stops? He said he never thinks about that, because the whole point of a technical barrier is that the market shouldn't go there if he is right. He tries to avoid points floor traders can reach easily — sometimes placing his stop beyond an obvious point, at a level he judges too far away or too difficult to reach easily. {M08}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "SAME RISK, TWO WAYS" (hypothetical). Budget: $1,000 at risk. Left: tight stop 1 point × 10 contracts — stopped out by noise, then price rises (grey). Right: wide stop 5 points × 2 contracts — survives the noise, rides the move (gold). Both lose $1,000 if wrong.

Schwager drew the moral, and it is one of the most useful ideas in the whole book. Place your stop where, if it is reached, the trade is reasonably shown to be wrong — not at a point set by how many dollars you are willing to lose per contract. If that meaningful stop implies an uncomfortably large loss per contract, trade fewer contracts. {M08}

Look at the hypothetical example. Both traders risk the same thousand dollars. One uses a tight stop and ten contracts, and is shaken out by ordinary noise before the market moves his way. The other uses a stop beyond a real barrier and only two contracts. Same risk. Very different chance of surviving long enough to be right. {A06}

[VISUAL] Small card: "Stops: 'never on the floor — but not mental'".

Because of his size, he didn't leave stop orders with the floor brokers. But, he insisted, his stops weren't mental either. He had organised his life so that the stops got taken care of. {M09}

And when trades go wrong repeatedly? A loss of money itself slows him down, he said, so he reduces his positions. And if the technical picture changes — if, for example, he is bearish on the dollar and a major intermediate high is broken — he has to rethink his view. {M10}

## 10 · CHAPTER NINE — ONE PERCENT

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "CHAPTER NINE · One Percent".

How much does he risk? Kovner's answer would surprise most beginners. He tries very hard not to risk more than one percent of his portfolio on any single trade. {M11}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "TEN LOSSES IN A ROW" (illustrative arithmetic). Four equity bars after 10 straight losses: 1% risk → 90% left · 2% → 82% · 5% → 60% · 10% → 35%.

The arithmetic shows why. Lose one percent ten times in a row and you still have about ninety percent of your capital. Lose ten percent ten times in a row and you are left with about a third — and you need to almost triple what remains just to get back to where you started. {A05}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "EIGHT POSITIONS, ONE BET". Eight small position blocks (e.g., marks, francs, guilders, sterling…) each "1% risk" — a correlation line links them; they merge into one large block: "≈ 8% — one idea". Then a pair: "LONG YEN / SHORT MARK".

Second, he studies how his trades are correlated. Every day, his firm ran a computer analysis to see how closely his positions moved together. Through bitter experience, he said, he learned that a mistake in correlation is the root of some of the most serious problems in trading. If you have eight highly correlated positions, you are really trading one position eight times as large. {M11}{A07}

So he preferred to trade one market against a related one. At the time of the interview, although he was net short the dollar, he was long the Japanese yen and short the Deutsche mark. In all his trading, he said: if I am long something, I like to be short something else. {M11}

[VISUAL] Data card: "1981 · −16% (Kovner's account) · first major commodity bear market". Explainer: bear-market price path — sharp drops, quick rebounds; a late SELL arrow stopped out in a choppy range.

He learned much of this from his one losing year. In 1981, by his account, he lost about sixteen percent. It was the first major bear market in commodities he had experienced, and bear markets behave differently from bull markets. {C31}

Bear markets, he found, are made of very sharp down moves followed by quick retracements. He kept selling too late, then getting stopped out in what turned out to be a wide, choppy range. In a bear market, he concluded, you have to use sharp rallies against the trend to enter positions. {M16}

His money management that year was poor, he said. He had too many correlated trades. So he went back to the drawing board and designed risk-management systems. He paid strict attention to the correlation of all his positions. From then on, he measured his total risk in the market every day. {C31}{M11}

[VISUAL] Lesson card: "The first rule of trading: don't get caught in a situation where you can lose a great deal of money for reasons you don't understand."

Which brings us back to the rule he called the first rule of trading — while admitting there are probably many first rules. Don't get caught in a situation in which you can lose a great deal of money for reasons you don't understand. {M12}

## 11 · CHAPTER TEN — THE WORLD NEVER SLEEPS

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "CHAPTER TEN · The World Never Sleeps". World map at night: Tokyo, Hong Kong, Zurich, London, New York light in sequence.

By 1988, currencies were the heart of Kovner's business. On average, he said, fifty to sixty percent of his profits came from currency trading. {C32}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "FUTURES vs INTERBANK". Left: exchange contract — fixed size, fixed date. Right: bank deal — any amount, any date, any pair, 24 hours. Example tag: "Hedge $3.6 million for 12 April — the bank just takes it".

He traded almost entirely in the interbank market — the network of banks dealing directly with each other — rather than on the futures exchange in Chicago. The reasons were practical: much better liquidity, much lower costs, and a market open twenty-four hours a day. A bank can tailor a deal to any amount and any date. A futures contract comes only in fixed sizes and fixed months. {C32}

And in the interbank market he could trade crosses: one foreign currency directly against another, such as the British pound against the Deutsche mark. Fewer people paid attention to the crosses, he said. And his general rule was simple: the less observed, the better the trade. {M17}

[VISUAL] Chart (stylised from Kovner's figures): sterling vs Deutsche mark in a band 2.96–3.00; a ceiling labelled "Bank of England defends 3.00"; break; a gap "3.01 → 3.035 — no trades"; climb to 3.11. Data card: "Early 1987–March 1988 — unannounced policy holding sterling near DM 3.00". Source: Bank of England history (Cambridge UP); Thatcher archive.

His favourite recent example came from sterling. For about a year, the pound had been trapped against the mark between roughly two ninety-six and three marks. The Bank of England kept defending the three-mark ceiling. We now know why: from early 1987 until March 1988, British policy was quietly to hold sterling near three Deutsche marks. {C33}

When the ceiling finally broke, Kovner said, something unusual happened. Above three point zero one, there were no trades at all until three point zero three five — a move of almost a full percent without a single deal. Everybody had been watching the three-mark level. Once they realised the Bank of England was not stepping in, no one wanted to sell. After a couple of hours of trading near three point zero four, the rate went straight up to three point one one. {C33}

Was that kind of violent breakout more reliable than a typical one? Much more, he said. Even with terrible fills? Especially then. The worse the fills, he said, the better the trade. {M17}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "THE CRASH-WEEK SCENARIOS". Week of 19 Oct 1987. Picture A: "Panic → dollar as safe haven" (Tuesday: dollar jumps). Picture B: "Stimulus needed · Germany & Japan won't inflate · U.S. trade deficit → let the dollar fall" (Friday: elements click). Data card: "Dollar ≈ −8% vs D-mark, Oct–mid-Nov 1987 · ≈ ¥122 by end-December". Source: EEC central bank governors' minutes; RBA data.

But his most vivid example of the scenario method came in the week of the 1987 crash. On the Friday after the nineteenth of October, he couldn't sleep — unusual for him. All week he had been trying on different visions of the world. {C35}

In one, the world was ending, financially. In that picture, the dollar becomes the safest haven, and soars. And on the Tuesday of crash week, the dollar did rise dramatically. For the next three days there was tremendous confusion. By the end of the week, the dollar had started to give ground again. {C35}

Then, he said, it all came together. The world needed stimulus after a financial panic. Germany's and Japan's central banks were reluctant to take measures that might stoke inflation. America's trade deficit remained wide. Someone had to play the stimulating role — and it would be the United States. The only solution, he concluded, was for Treasury Secretary James Baker to let the dollar go. {C35}

The record supports the logic of that view. Baker had publicly criticised West Germany's rising interest rates and signalled that Washington might tolerate a lower dollar. In the weeks after the crash, the dollar fell about eight percent against the mark, and by the end of December it had dropped to around a hundred and twenty-two yen. {C35}

It was too late to act on Friday. It was a very tense weekend, he said, because the dollar might open sharply lower. He waited for the markets of the Far East to open on Sunday night. {C35}

[VISUAL] Clock ring: 8 A.M. → 7 P.M. (New York) · 8 P.M. Tokyo opens. Card: "Call levels in every currency · Written scenario for every currency, every week".

That was how he lived. He had trading monitors at home and in his country house, and staff on duty twenty-four hours a day. Every currency had call levels; if one broke out of its expected range, someone called him. Once a week, the team wrote a scenario for every currency: the range they expected, and what they would do if it broke. His assistant, he joked, was allowed to wake him up twice a year. {M29}

Why live like that? Because, he said, it didn't feel like work — except when you lose. For him, market analysis was a tremendous multidimensional chess board. Trying to work out what the finance minister of New Zealand would do next about his country's problems wasn't exotic, he said. It was tremendous fun. {M28}

## 12 · CHAPTER ELEVEN — THE ELEPHANT

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "CHAPTER ELEVEN · The Elephant". Data card: "1988 · Over $650 million managed · ≈ $300 million profit in 1987 (Kovner's account)".

By 1988, Kovner was managing over six hundred and fifty million dollars. His profits in 1987 alone, he said, were about three hundred million. {C30}{C28}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "THE ELEPHANT IN THE ROOM". Two pools: COPPER (daily volume ≈ 7,000–10,000 contracts) and T-BONDS. A block "500–800 contracts" barely fits in copper (large ripple); "5,000 contracts" disappears into bonds. Figures: Kovner's estimates.

Size changed everything. There were far fewer markets with enough liquidity for the trades he needed. In copper, he said, he could comfortably move five hundred to eight hundred contracts a day — when the entire daily volume was only seven to ten thousand, much of it local traders and spreads. In Treasury bonds, he could move five thousand contracts without a problem. "In copper," he said, "I am now the elephant." {C36}

Even a good trade in a small market barely mattered. He had made a few million dollars in coffee the year before. But when you manage six hundred million, he said, the time and energy spent on coffee can take your focus away from the currency markets that really drive results. {C37}

Could he simply push smaller markets around? He never bullies a market, he said. It can work in the short term, but eventually it leads to serious mistakes: arrogance, and a loss of touch with the underlying market. Traders he had known who thought too highly of their ability and tried to bully markets ended up overtrading — and went under. {M14}

He gave a recent example without naming names: a British trading organisation that tried to corner the crude-oil market. At first, he said, they succeeded. Then they lost control, prices fell, and they lost around forty million dollars. We have not been able to identify the firm, and we will not guess. {C38}

[VISUAL] Lesson card: "If you personalise these losses, you can't trade."

Size also brought an emotional weight. On any given day, he said, he could lose millions of dollars. If you personalise those losses, you can't trade. {M15}

The only thing that still disturbed him was poor money management — taking a loss that was much too large. As long as losses came from sound technique, the process of losing money didn't bother him at all. {M15}

## 13 · CHAPTER TWELVE — THE RULES

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "CHAPTER TWELVE · The Rules". Each rule appears as a numbered card in gold.

So what would he tell a beginner? {M23}

**Rule one: risk management comes first.** Before anything else, he said, it is the most important thing to understand. {M23}

**Rule two: undertrade, undertrade, undertrade.** Whatever you think your position ought to be, cut it at least in half. {M23}

[VISUAL] Explainer — "THE NOVICE'S SIZE PROBLEM". Bar: "Novice risk per trade 5–10%" vs "Recommended 1–2%". Multiplier label: "3–5× too big".

In his experience, novice traders trade three to five times too big. They take five to ten percent risks on a single trade, when they should be taking one or two. {M23}

**Rule three: don't personalise the market.** A common mistake, he said, is to treat the market as a personal enemy. The market is totally impersonal. It doesn't care whether you make money. Whenever a trader says "I wish" or "I hope," he is thinking destructively — because it takes attention away from the diagnosis. {M24}

[VISUAL] Data card: "≈ 30 trainees → 4–5 good traders (Kovner's account)". Three words: STRONG · INDEPENDENT · CONTRARY.

Can trading be taught? Only to a limited extent, he said. Over the years he had tried to train perhaps thirty people. Only four or five became good traders. The others left the business — and it had nothing to do with intelligence. {C39}

The ones who made it, he said, were "strong, independent, and contrary in the extreme." They could take positions others were unwilling to take. And they were disciplined enough to take the right size. {M25}

**Rule four: greed is fatal.** "A greedy trader always blows out," he said. He described one colleague at Commodities Corporation as a brilliant trader, with wonderful ideas, who often picked the right markets and knew them better than Kovner did. Yet Kovner kept money, and he did not. The reason was position size. For every contract Kovner traded, that man traded ten. He would double his money twice a year — and still end up flat. {M25}{C40}

**Rule five: work hard — and beware the one-year wonder.** Trading is close to a zero-sum game, he said, so by definition only a small group can be superior. Without very hard work, it is extremely unlikely you will be a good trader. There are plenty of one-year wonders: someone convinced sugar is going to forty cents, or that copper spreads must widen, who turns out right once. He had recently heard of a trader who made twenty-seven million dollars in copper spreads in a year — and then lost virtually all of it. {M22}{C41}

**Rule six: hold your own conviction.** Kovner read a daily "guru report" summarising the big newsletter writers. He didn't simply follow them; he looked for a consensus the market wasn't confirming — a sign that many people were about to be wrong. Could you profit just by following gurus? Probably, he said, but making money requires holding a position with conviction, and that is very hard when the idea belongs to someone else. {M26}

## 14 · CHAPTER THIRTEEN — WAS HE RIGHT?

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "CHAPTER THIRTEEN · Was He Right?". Analysis segment — on-screen label: "ANALYSIS · OUR INTERPRETATION".

In 1988, Kovner made several judgments that we can now check against history.

[VISUAL] Data card: "19 Oct 1987 — Dow −508 points (−22.6%)". Source: Encyclopedia.com. Card: "Brady Report, Jan 1988 — portfolio-insurance selling a major factor". Counterpoint: "Shiller (1988): plausible, but contested". Sources: Brady Report; NBER.

First, the crash. He blamed two things: stock prices that had climbed too high, and heavy selling by pension funds using so-called portfolio insurance — a strategy of selling stock-index futures as prices fall. He called it "insurance in name only": in effect, a massive stop-loss order. The government's Brady Report reached a similar conclusion in January 1988. Later economists, including Robert Shiller, argued it was a plausible cause but not a proven one. The debate continues; his diagnosis sits within the mainstream view. {M21}{C43}{C44}

[VISUAL] Chart (stylised): dollar/yen from ≈ ¥125 (1988) to the first break below ¥100 (21 Jun 1994) and ¥79.75 (19 Apr 1995). Source: contemporary reports. Marker: "Kovner, 1988: 'the dollar can fall to 100 yen'".

Second, the dollar. When he spoke of imagining the dollar at a hundred yen, it was trading around a hundred and twenty to a hundred and thirty. That world arrived six years later. In June 1994, the dollar fell below a hundred yen for the first time since the war, and in April 1995 it touched seventy-nine point seven five. Imagining it was not the same as trading it — but his example was not fantasy. {C54}{A11}

Third, a bold forecast. Kovner believed that, without a period of high inflation, simple trend-following systems would kill each other off under the weight of their own size and similarity. Here we offer interpretation, not fact. Trend-following funds did not disappear; they kept operating for decades and had powerful years when large moves appeared. But his underlying point — that crowded signals become less reliable — is still widely accepted. {M19}{A10}

[VISUAL] Data card: "Caxton · 1983–2011". Lines: "≈ 28% a year for 18 years to 2003" · "2003: returned ≈ 20% of capital — size concerns" · "single digits after 2003" · "≈ 21% a year after fees over 1983–2011". Sources: Institutional Investor (2011); Bloomberg.

And what about Kovner himself? He kept trading at scale for more than two decades. According to Institutional Investor, Caxton averaged about twenty-eight percent a year in the eighteen years up to 2003. That year, the firm handed back about a fifth of its investors' capital, worried that its size was hurting returns. Afterwards, returns averaged in single digits. Over his whole run from 1983 to 2011, Bloomberg calculates about twenty-one percent a year after fees. {C45}{C46}{C47}

[VISUAL] Disclosure card: "1977–1988 figures as reported by Kovner and Schwager. Later Caxton figures as reported in financial press; no audited record is publicly available."

Two observations. First, the figures from 1977 to 1988 come from Kovner and Schwager; no independent audited record is public. Second, the later decline fits something Kovner said in 1988: size reduces the number of markets you can trade well. The elephant in copper was a warning about the future. {A09}

[VISUAL] Two-column card. STRENGTHS: scenario thinking · humility about what the market knows · stops beyond real barriers · 1% risk & correlation control · adaptability. WEAKNESSES: method hard to copy · depends on a global information network · size limits opportunity · one impulsive decision nearly ended his career.

His approach had clear strengths: imagination disciplined by evidence; respect for what price is telling you; stops set where the idea is proven wrong; tiny risk per trade; and attention to correlation. It also had limits. As Schwager admitted, a synthesis of worldwide economics on this scale is hardly translatable to the average trader. It depended on an extraordinary network of contacts and staff. And as his money grew, it ran into the hard ceiling of liquidity.

What remains relevant today? Almost all of the risk rules. Decide the stop before the entry. Let the stop set the size. Risk one or two percent, not ten. Count correlated positions as one bet. Ask whether a breakout is crowded or quiet. And when something happens that you don't understand, step aside first and think later.

## 15 · EPILOGUE

[VISUAL] Chapter card: "EPILOGUE". Slow fade to warm charcoal. Data card: "Retired from Caxton · 2011 · Andrew Law succeeds". Source: Bloomberg; Wikipedia — Caxton Associates.

In 2011, Bruce Kovner retired from Caxton and handed the firm to Andrew Law, whom he had named chief investment officer in 2008. He set up a private firm to manage his own money. {C48}{C49}

[VISUAL] Data card: "Chairman, Juilliard School board · 2001–2022 · Feb 2006 — gift of ≈ 140 music manuscripts (Bach, Mozart, Beethoven, Brahms)". Sources: Juilliard; NPR.

Away from the markets, he became one of America's leading supporters of classical music. He chaired the board of the Juilliard School for twenty-one years. In 2006, he gave Juilliard a collection of around a hundred and forty music manuscripts and annotated editions — Bach, Mozart, Beethoven, Brahms. He declined to put his name on it. It is simply the Juilliard Manuscript Collection. He also became a major donor to education and to public-policy institutions, including chairing the board of the American Enterprise Institute. {C50}{C51}{C52}

For most of his career, he refused interviews. He told Schwager he agreed to this one because the stories about him were usually distorted and fanciful, and he wanted to establish at least one accurate record. {C29}

[VISUAL] Return to the image from the cold open: the soybean board, frozen at LIMIT DOWN. It unfreezes; the November row reappears beside July — a balanced spread.

Look back at that April morning in 1977. The young trader was right about the shortage. He was right about the spread. He even got out in time. What nearly ruined him was a single impulsive yes — giving up his protection in a moment of excitement.

What made Bruce Kovner a Market Wizard was that he never forgot it. He spent the rest of his career imagining every possible world — and making sure none of them could destroy him.
